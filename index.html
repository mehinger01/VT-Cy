🎯 Today's Mission

Building the thinking habits that lead to success in mathematics.

Today we are collecting evidence about how you solve problems — not just whether you get the right answer.

We'll practice:

  • Looking carefully
  • Building ideas with objects
  • Explaining our thinking
  • Drawing our ideas
  • Connecting those ideas to mathematics

Every challenge helps us discover strengths and decide what to work on next.

🔍 Investigation 1: Notice

Academic Skills

  • Pattern recognition
  • Visual reasoning
  • Comparing information

Student Success Skills

  • Looking carefully
  • Explaining ideas
  • Staying flexible
Notice and Wonder

Look at this arrangement:

🔵🔵🔵   🔵🔵🔵🔵🔵   🔵🔵

What do you notice? What do you wonder?

Try looking at the groups without counting one at a time — what do you see right away?
There are three groups of circles. One group has more than the others, and one has the fewest. Noticing the groups first (before counting) is a strategy mathematicians use.
Spot the Pattern

2, 4, 8, 16, __. What comes next? How do you know?

Look at what's happening between each number — is it adding the same amount, or something else?
Each number is doubling: 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32. Explaining the rule in words ("it doubles each time") is just as important as getting the next number.
Compare the Groups

Group A: 🟩🟩🟩🟩🟩🟩🟩   Group B: 🟨🟨🟨🟨🟨

Which group has more? How can you tell without counting one by one?

Could you match one square from each group to a partner? What's left over tells you something.
Group A has more — if you match them up one-to-one, Group A has 2 extra squares with no partner. That's a quick way to compare without counting everything.

🏗️ Investigation 2: Build

Academic Skills

  • Number sense
  • Comparison
  • Equal groups

Student Success Skills

  • Planning before acting
  • Testing ideas
  • Trying more than one strategy
Build a Number Two Ways

Using objects (blocks, counters, or coins), build the number 24. Then build it again a completely different way.

Think about tens and ones — could you also build it in groups of 2s, or one big pile plus a few more?
One way: 2 groups of 10 plus 4 ones. Another way: 12 groups of 2, or 24 single objects grouped by color. Any accurate way of building 24 counts — the goal is flexibility.
Equal Groups Challenge

Build 3 equal groups with 5 objects in each group. How many objects are there in all?

Could you count all the objects one at a time, or is there a faster way once the groups are built?
3 groups of 5 is 15 objects total. Counting by 5s (5, 10, 15) is faster than counting one at a time — that's the beginning of multiplication thinking.
Build & Compare

Build two towers: one with 9 blocks and one with 6 blocks. How many more blocks does the taller tower have? Show it with the blocks, not just on paper.

Try lining the towers up side by side, block by block. What's left uncovered?
The 9-block tower has 3 more blocks than the 6-block tower. Lining them up shows the "difference" physically — it's the same idea as subtraction.

✏️ Investigation 3: Draw

Academic Skills

  • Visual models
  • Addition and subtraction stories
  • Equal groups

Student Success Skills

  • Making thinking visible
  • Organizing information
  • Slowing down before solving
Draw the Story

Maya had 14 stickers. She gave 6 to her friend. Draw a picture showing what happened, then figure out how many stickers Maya has left.

Try drawing all 14 stickers first, then cross out the ones Maya gave away.
Maya has 8 stickers left. A picture with 14 stickers, 6 crossed out, and 8 remaining shows the whole story — not just the answer.
Draw Equal Groups

Draw 4 groups with 3 stars in each group. How many stars are there in all?

Circling each group of 3 as you draw it can help you keep track of how many groups you've made.
4 groups of 3 stars is 12 stars total. Drawing the groups clearly (circled or spaced apart) makes it easy to count by 3s.
Draw to Compare

Draw two groups of objects so that one group has exactly 5 more than the other.

Start with the smaller group, then figure out exactly how many to add to make it 5 more.
Any pair works as long as the difference is 5 — for example, 4 and 9, or 10 and 15. Lining the two groups up side by side shows the extra 5 clearly.

💭 Investigation 4: Reason

Academic Skills

  • Missing information
  • Relevant information
  • Early algebra thinking

Student Success Skills

  • Explaining reasoning
  • Noticing what matters
  • Working backward
What's Missing?

Jordan bought some notebooks and had $12 left over. How much money did Jordan start with?

What would you need to know to answer this — how much the notebooks cost, or how many Jordan bought?
This problem is missing information — we don't know how much the notebooks cost or how many were bought, so it can't be solved yet. Noticing that something is missing is the important skill here.
Too Much Information

Sam is 11 years old and has brown hair. Sam bought 3 packs of trading cards with 8 cards in each pack. How many trading cards does Sam have?

Which details actually help you find the number of cards, and which ones don't matter at all?
Sam's age and hair color don't matter — only "3 packs of 8 cards" matters. 3 × 8 = 24 trading cards. Spotting irrelevant details is just as important as using the relevant ones.
Work Backward

Sam ends the day with 9 marbles, after giving away 4 marbles earlier. How many marbles did Sam start with?

If you undo the "giving away" step, would you add or subtract to work backward?
Sam started with 13 marbles (9 + 4 = 13). Working backward from the ending point is a powerful reasoning strategy, especially when the starting amount is unknown.

🔢 Investigation 5: Connect to Math

Academic Skills

  • Number sentences
  • Missing addends
  • Equal groups
  • Difference/comparison

Student Success Skills

  • Connecting models to symbols
  • Checking if answers make sense
  • Explaining strategy
Write the Number Sentence

Remember Maya's 14 stickers from the Draw investigation? Write a number sentence that matches her story.

Maya started with 14 and gave some away — which operation matches "giving away"?
14 − 6 = 8. Turning the picture from the Draw investigation into a number sentence shows how a model connects to symbols.
Missing Addend

7 + ___ = 15. What number goes in the blank? Explain how you figured it out.

How far apart are 7 and 15? Counting up from 7 to 15 might help.
The missing number is 8, because 7 + 8 = 15. Counting up from 7 to 15, or subtracting 15 − 7, both get you there — either strategy is valid.
Equal Groups as Multiplication

4 groups of 3. Write it as repeated addition, then as a multiplication sentence.

Repeated addition means adding the same number over and over — how many times would you add 3?
Repeated addition: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. Multiplication: 4 × 3 = 12. Both number sentences describe the same 4 equal groups.
Comparison Number Sentence

One tower has 9 blocks, the other has 6 blocks. Write a subtraction sentence that shows the difference.

"Difference" means how many more one has than the other — which operation shows that?
9 − 6 = 3. This matches the Build investigation, where lining up the towers physically showed the same difference of 3.
